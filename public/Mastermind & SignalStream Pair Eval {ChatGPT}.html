## Vetting a revenue partner: time‑tested best practices

The fastest way partnerships go sideways is when *expectations are implicit* and *incentives are misaligned*. The antidote is boring (and effective): **define the relationship, run a work trial, then codify the rules while everyone still likes each other.**

### 1) Start by naming the partnership type (it changes the vetting)
- **Co-founder / equity partner** (high trust, long horizon; hard to unwind)
- **Channel/affiliate partner** (distribution + rev share; easier to unwind)
- **Build partner** (dev/design/ops; usually paid + limited rev share)
- **Strategic partner** (integrations, bundles, community partnerships; shared brand risk)

YC frames cofounding as a *very* long relationship (think years), so you’re vetting for durability—not just immediate skill fit.

### 2) Use a 3‑layer vetting stack: Character → Competence → Compatibility
**A. Character (non‑negotiable)**
- Integrity under pressure (ask for examples of a mistake + how they handled it)
- Reputation checks (2–3 references: “Would you partner with them again?”)
- Money honesty (how they handle rev splits, refunds, chargebacks, ambiguity)
- “Paper comfort” (do they welcome clear agreements or dodge them?)

**B. Competence (proof, not claims)**
- Ask for *artifacts*: past launches, funnels, landing pages, partnership deals, audience metrics
- Look for repeatable behavior: shipping cadence, follow-through, “closing loops”

**C. Compatibility (how you *work*)**
- Decision style: fast vs consensus
- Conflict style: avoidant vs direct
- Risk tolerance: “Are we okay with a 0-revenue month to learn?”
- Time/energy reality: weekly hours, response times, availability windows

### 3) Run a “work trial” before anything irreversible
YC’s cofounder guidance is blunt: you only really learn by working together, so do a trial and decide upfront what happens to the work if you part ways.

A good work-trial for SignalStream partnerships:
- **Duration:** 2–4 weeks  
- **Goal:** produce one measurable outcome (e.g., “200 waitlist signups” or “2 creator pilots live”)
- **Scope:** one channel + one offer + one landing page variant
- **Exit terms:** who owns what, what can be reused, what must be removed

### 4) Formalize the partnership like adults (even if you’re friends)
Not legal advice—just practical reality:

**Minimum viable partner agreement**
- Scope + deliverables (what exactly they do, by when)
- Revenue share definition (gross vs net; payout timing; refunds/chargebacks)
- Attribution + tracking (UTM rules, promo codes, dashboard screenshots)
- Brand usage guidelines (what can/can’t be claimed publicly)
- IP ownership (who owns landing pages, creative, copy, automations)
- Confidentiality + data handling
- Breakup plan (termination, wind-down, who keeps what, non-disparagement)

Also: partner due diligence is a recognized risk-management practice because partners can expose you to reputational/financial risk.

### 5) Red flags that deserve a hard “no”
- Won’t do a trial, wants long-term commitment immediately
- Avoids written terms (“trust me”)
- Inflates metrics, won’t show receipts
- Can’t name past failures (or blames everyone else)
- Pushes hype-y promises (especially around revenue) without clear mechanisms

## Aristotle’s 3 friendships: how much does it apply to business partnerships?

Aristotle distinguishes **three kinds of friendship**: based on **utility**, **pleasure**, and **virtue**.

Here’s the *business-relevant* interpretation:

### 1) Utility friendship = most partnerships
A business partnership is usually a “friendship of utility”: each party benefits, and it endures as long as the mutual benefit endures. Aristotle explicitly treats utility-based relationships as real but often less stable than virtue-based ones.

**How to use this lens**
- Ask: “If the utility changes (pivot, slower growth, different channel), do we still have a relationship—or does it collapse?”
- Design contracts assuming utility will change.

### 2) Pleasure friendship = networking + creative chemistry
You *like* each other, it’s energizing, you collaborate easily.
- This is powerful for creator/community work—chemistry matters.
- But chemistry alone won’t survive uneven workload or unclear money.

### 3) Virtue friendship = the rare foundation for enduring partnerships
Virtue friendship is grounded in mutual respect for character and shared values. It tends to be more durable because it’s not purely transactional.

**Where it helps you most**
- Trust under ambiguity (early-stage startups are ambiguity machines)
- Fairness when attribution is fuzzy
- Resilience after a failed experiment

**The key nuance:** virtue-friendship can *support* a business partnership, but it should not *replace* clear agreements. The most durable business partnerships often have:
- Utility alignment (clear win-win)  
- Plus virtue traits (trust + goodwill)  
- Plus written terms (clarity + protection)

YC’s “would I want to be friends with this person?” heuristic is basically Aristotle-compatible: you’re screening for something beyond utility.

Creators (and platforms) won’t mostly object because “people learned something without watching.” They’ll object when it feels like:

1) **their monetization is being bypassed**,

### Counter #1: Most “saved” content is never watched anyway
A large portion of long-form content exists in a user’s “I’ll get to it” queue. SignalStream can convert *non-consumption* into *partial consumption + action*.

That’s why your positioning is strong: “Summaries compress everything equally… SignalStream ruthlessly extracts only what matters to your goals…”

## 3) The biggest opposition holes in your idea (as it stands)

### Hole #1: Platform policy risk (not just creator feelings)
If SignalStream relies on automated transcript retrieval at scale, the *platform* may object before creators ever do.

Your workflow currently prompts a model: “Provide the full spoken-word transcript for this YouTube video… Video URL: …”

But YouTube’s Terms restrict automated access (robots/scrapers) and reproduction/use of content except as authorized, and explicitly allow showing videos via the embeddable player.
That means:
- you’ll want to be very careful about *how* transcripts are sourced
- and you’ll want to bias toward **embedding + linking** as the canonical consumption path

Also, YouTube’s ToS forbids selling ads/sponsorships on pages where YouTube content is the primary draw (with limited exceptions).
So if SignalStream ever becomes “a page full of embedded YouTube videos + your own ads,” that’s a potential conflict.

This is a bigger “opposition” vector than individual creator complaints.

### Hole #2: Misrepresentation risk (the “AI put words in my mouth” problem)
Creators will tolerate “summaries” far more than they tolerate:
- wrong claims,
- missing nuance,
- fabricated “key takeaways” attributed to them.

Your system creates a **Golden Nugget** and structured extraction automatically.
That’s powerful — but it increases the probability of accidental distortion.

Mitigation that actually matters:
- show *quotes* sparingly, but accurately
- link every claim back to source
- include “context needed” flags
- allow creators to claim their page and correct/curate

### Hole #3: The “psychological profiles” feature could trigger backlash
“Psychological Profiles” is marketed as: “Understand the success patterns… What traits and mental models drive their results?”

Some creators will love it. Others will see it as:
- armchair diagnosis
- brand risk
- creepy / invasive

If you want fewer problems:
- make it **opt-in**
- rename it to something less charged (e.g., “Communication Patterns” or “Decision Style”)
- keep it anchored to *observable content*, not personality labels

### Hole #4: Your most creator-hostile interpretation is also your most user-attractive

## 4) Realistically: will creators oppose this, or be in favor?

### What I’d expect in practice
Most creators won’t “oppose” in a loud way unless:
- you become big enough to notice, **and**
- they feel you’re *extracting value without giving any back*.

**Phase 1 (small):**  
- creators mostly don’t notice
- the ones you talk to directly will be mixed: curious + cautious

**Phase 2 (growing):**  
- some creators become *actively supportive* if you drive measurable discovery/conversions
- a minority becomes *actively resistant* if they perceive cannibalization or misrepresentation

**Phase 3 (meaningful scale):**  
- platform policy becomes the main constraint (especially for YouTube-style sources)
- creator partnerships become a moat (permission + distribution)

### Who is most likely to be supportive
- educational creators who already rely on “implementation” rather than “watch time”
- creators selling products/services where *leads matter more than ads*
- creators who want their ideas spread (and are confident in their brand)

### Who is most likely to push back
- creators heavily optimized for watch time/ad revenue
- creators whose “value” is performance/entertainment (summaries are substitutional)
- creators with paywalled or premium content ecosystems
- creators who’ve previously been misquoted/misrepresented

So: **expect some opposition eventually** — but it’s not “everyone will hate it.”
